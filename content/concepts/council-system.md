# Council System

> Council system names Arendt's recurring revolutionary form of local, participatory self-government. In [[on-revolution|On Revolution]] it is the revolution's "lost treasure": a genuine experience of public freedom that revolutions keep rediscovering — spontaneously, without coordination — and that parties, bureaucracy, and the centralized state keep displacing. It is a *republican* concept, not an anarcho-capitalist one, and the wiki holds it there deliberately.

## The Council Against the Party

For Arendt the councils are not advisory committees or interest groups; they are **spaces of action** — the places where ordinary people, after liberation, actually govern themselves by deliberating and deciding in concert. That makes them the concrete home of the [[public-happiness|public happiness]] the revolution was fought for: the tangible experience of being a participant in public affairs rather than a private consumer of a government's outputs. Their rival is the *party*. Where a council is direct participation, a party is representation and administration — it acts *for* the people rather than letting the people act. Arendt's sharp claim is that the two are not complementary but antagonistic: the genuine revolutions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries throw up councils as organs of freedom and then hand power to parties, which treat the councils as competitors to be dissolved. Her claim is not that this happens in literally every uprising — she expressly sets the February Revolution of 1848 aside — but that it recurs wherever a revolution genuinely opens space for public action. The party system, on her reading, does not extend public freedom; it professionalizes politics and returns most people to private life.

## Foundation vs Liberation: Why the Councils Are Always Lost

The recurrence and the failure have the same root. The revolutionary tradition, Arendt argues, prized **liberation** — freedom *from* the old regime, tyranny, and want — over **foundation**, the positive task of constituting durable public spaces in which freedom can be exercised. Once liberation was won, the councils looked to the professional revolutionaries like leftover machinery rather than the point of the whole enterprise, and were disbanded as threats to the new central power. They were never given a constitutional home. Arendt's constitutional road not taken is Jefferson's **ward system** — his "elementary republics", meant to give every citizen a small, tangible arena of action so that the republic would rest on participation rather than only on periodic voting. Her tone throughout is sympathetic but candid: she recovers the council experience as real and repeatedly proven, without offering a worked-out institutional blueprint for making it last.

External suppression is not her only explanation, and it would misrepresent her to imply it were. She also holds that the councils failed at something they attempted themselves. Where they tried to take over the running of factories they did badly, and she thinks this was predictable: council members were chosen on political criteria — trustworthiness, integrity, judgement, sometimes physical courage — and the qualities of the political actor are not those of the manager, being rarely found in the same person. The distinction is one she insists on throughout: politics deals with people in a field whose principle is freedom, administration with things in a sphere whose principle is necessity. Bringing the council's element of action into the management of things, on her account, could only create chaos — and it was these attempts that earned the councils much of their poor reputation. For this wiki the moral is pointed, since it is precisely the conflation of running things with governing people that the [[economic-calculation-problem|calculation debate]] attacks from the other direction.

## The Historical Instances

Arendt's cases are concrete and span the modern revolutionary record: the revolutionary societies and *sections* of the French Revolution; the Paris Commune of 1871; the Russian *soviets* of 1905 and 1917; the German workers' and soldiers' councils (*Räte*) of 1918–1919; and the neighborhood, revolutionary, worker, and student councils of the 1956 Hungarian Revolution. Across a century and a half, in unconnected settings, the same form arises unbidden — which is why she treats it not as one political program among others but as something the revolutionary spirit *discovers* whenever it opens space for public action.

## What Arendt Actually Proposes

Two features of her argument are routinely softened by sympathetic summaries, including an earlier version of this page. Both cut against reading the councils as a light-touch, maximally participatory alternative.

First, the councils are not an alternative to government. She describes them as the germs of a new state and of a new form of government — their quarrel is with party rule, bureaucratic administration and centralization, not with the existence of a body politic. Reading them as an anti-state idea inverts the argument: the tragedy she narrates is that the councils were never given a *constitutional* home, which is a complaint about state-building left undone.

Second — and this page previously got her attitude backwards — a fully developed council system would be pyramidal, which she says is the shape of an essentially authoritarian government. She does not present that as an embarrassment. Her point is that in every authoritarian government we know, authority is filtered down from above, whereas here it would be generated on each layer of the pyramid, and she suggests that this *could be the solution* to what she considers one of the most serious problems of modern politics: not how to reconcile freedom and equality, but how to reconcile equality and authority.

She accepts the consequence that follows. Such a form would end general suffrage as it is understood today, because only those who had shown, by volunteering into an elementary republic, that they care about more than their private happiness would have a say in the republic's business. She defends this as self-exclusion rather than imposed discrimination — those who do not belong have not been kept out by any outside body — and insists the resulting group is not a social, cultural or professional elite.

This is the part of the council idea a libertarian or democratic reader should weigh most carefully, and it should be weighed as what it is: a proposal she advances, not a difficulty she leaves hanging.

## Libertarian Caution

The council system should not be forced into [[market-anarchism-and-private-law|market anarchism]] or private-law theory. It is a republican, participatory idea, and its value to the wiki is precisely that it is *neither* market exchange *nor* party-and-bureaucratic administration: it gives a vocabulary for public action that neither the market nor the administrative state supplies. That is a narrower claim than calling it non-state — as above, Arendt's councils are a proposed form of government, not an escape from one. It belongs to the wiki's Arendt cluster in [[state-theory-and-totalitarianism|State Theory and Totalitarianism]] as the *positive* counterpart to her analysis of totalitarian world-destruction — the form of *acting in concert* [[violence-vs-power|power]] that totalitarian rule exists to annihilate. Where the libertarian tradition answers *how should people cooperate without the state?* with contract and exchange, Arendt answers a different question — *where is public freedom actually experienced?* — and the two answers are worth keeping distinct rather than collapsing into one.
