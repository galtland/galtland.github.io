# Flag Theory

> Flag theory is the practice of spreading the legal pieces of one's life (citizenship, tax residence, business, assets, and the places one actually lives and spends money) across several countries, so that no single government holds all of them. Harry Schultz's "Three Flag Theory" became, through books published under the name W. G. Hill, the creed of the *perpetual traveler* or PT. It is [[jurisdictional-competition|jurisdictional competition]] practiced from the demand side: instead of waiting for governments to compete for residents, the individual shops among them. In both its 1990s and current forms, the point is less to keep moving than never to be trapped.

## Origins: Schultz's Three Flags

Harry Schultz, a Milwaukee-born investment newsletter writer, traced the idea to his years as a soldier in Shanghai during the Second World War, where he "learned what it was to be under the thumb of a Big Brother Bureaucracy." After selling a string of American newspapers he took a year-long trip around the world and never moved back to the United States permanently. He wrote his newsletter, founded in 1964, from wherever he happened to be, and described his base not as a place but as a "floating" home: "Not a boat, but rather a state of mind that could allow me to be a sort of modern-day nomad." In a mini-book, *How to Keep Your Money and Your Freedom*, written about twenty years before Hill's book appeared, he set out the original formula:

> "Three Flags" was the way I described the need to have a second passport, a safe haven for your assets outside your own country and a legal address in a tax haven.
>
> — Harry Schultz, interviewed in W. G. Hill, *PT*

Around the same time he met Bill Hill and, with him, "expanded the three flags to five. We included a place of business and playgrounds." Hill, for his part, called Schultz's pamphlet "my greatest discovery." The resulting book, *PT* (around 1989), was published by Scope International of Waterlooville, England, under a name that may have been a pseudonym; it went through as many as seven editions, sold through classified advertising in *The Times*, and was followed in 1993 by *PT2*.

## The Five Flags

In the five-flag version, each of the following sits in a different country:

- **Passport and citizenship**: from a country that does not tax income earned abroad or control what its citizens do.
- **Legal tax residence**: in a tax haven.
- **Business base**: where money is earned, ideally with low corporate tax.
- **Asset haven**: where money is kept, ideally with low taxes on passive income and capital gains.
- **Playgrounds**: where money is spent, ideally with low consumption taxes.

The financial commentator Bob Beckman gave the short form in 1995, explaining why he lived in Monaco: the efficient way to handle one's affairs was "to work one place, keep his money in a second place and live in a third place." Later writers kept adding flags, and some versions count seven. Emile Phaneuf III and Rahim Taghizadegan summarize flag theory as "arranging your affairs to optimize for a combination of the best governance (with favorable tax rates playing an important part of that) and lifestyle."

## Sovereignty as Paperwork

The early literature framed the PT as a sovereign in miniature. Adam Starchild, writing in 1995, said the concept "could have been called Individual Sovereignty, because PTs look after themselves." He described its mechanism as administrative rather than physical:

> In a nutshell, a PT merely arranges his or her "paperwork" in such a way that all governments consider him a tourist -- a person who is just "passing through."
>
> — Adam Starchild, “PT Philosophy & Practice”

The initials could stand for "prior taxpayer," "perpetual tourist," or "permanent traveler", but travel itself was optional: "The individual who is a PT can stay in one place most of the time. Or all of the time." What mattered was holding the option. Starchild also set the PT apart from projects that try to build new polities, such as [[seasteading-and-network-states|seasteading and network states]]: "You don't need to found a new country or displace someone else to make yourself a sovereign." Flag theory uses the jurisdictions that already exist and plays them against each other.

Outside descriptions put motion at the center instead. Wikipedia's article defines the PT as someone who bases different parts of life in different countries "without spending too long in any one place", so that no country treats them as a legal resident.

## Why Mobility Is the Lever

The theory behind flag theory is that immobility is what makes people taxable and controllable. [[franz-oppenheimer|Oppenheimer]] located the origin of the state in the conquest of settled farmers, who were tied to their fields and hard to rally even for common defense (see [[oppenheimer-on-conquest|Oppenheimer on conquest]]):

> The peasant is always lacking in mobility. He is as attached to the ground as the plants he cultivates.
>
> — [[franz-oppenheimer|Franz Oppenheimer]], [[oppenheimer-the-state|The State]]

[[james-dale-davidson|Davidson]] and [[william-rees-mogg|Rees-Mogg]] apply the same logic to industry: factories "could not be moved", which made them "sitting ducks for coercive shakedowns". Their forecast is that information technology reverses this:

> Capital in the Information Age is growing more mobile by the moment. The capacity to earn high income is no longer tied to residence in specific locations, as was the case when most wealth was created by manipulating natural resources.
>
> — [[james-dale-davidson|James Dale Davidson]] and [[william-rees-mogg|William Rees-Mogg]], [[the-sovereign-individual|The Sovereign Individual]]

Phaneuf and Taghizadegan ground the practice in public-choice economics: Charles Tiebout's mobile "consumer-voters" choosing among local governments, Albert Hirschman's distinction between exit and voice, [[james-buchanan|James M. Buchanan]] on the uncertainty of the ballot compared with the market, and Ilya Somin's "foot voting". Scaled up from towns to countries, they describe a market:

> There is, in fact, a global marketplace for governance. States may monopolize governance within their own territories, but shopping among those monopolies opens up exciting possibilities.
>
> — Emile Phaneuf III and Rahim Taghizadegan, “Jurisdiction Shopping: The Forgotten Logic Behind Flag Theory”

They also trace an Austrian-school lineage. Schultz credited [[friedrich-a-hayek|Hayek]] as his main economic inspiration; [[carl-menger|Menger]], expecting a European war, moved wealth into gold and into securities in neutral Sweden; and [[ludwig-von-mises|Mises]] took a professorship in Geneva in 1934 before fleeing the Nazi threat, "reminding us that mobility is sometimes not optimization but survival."

Katie The Russian, who runs an investment-migration firm, puts the demand side bluntly: a person is born into "a nonconsensual monopoly", and since "Only about 3.6% of people live outside of the country of their origins", the governments of birth hold "more than 96% market domination."

## Flag Theory 2.0: Presence, Not Invisibility

The 1990s PT depended on governments not talking to each other. Ahmad Abbas, writing in an investment-migration trade publication in 2026, describes that model as "minimal footprint anywhere, perpetual motion to avoid establishing residence", and its end:

> That world died. Common Reporting Standard agreements, FATCA, and automatic information exchange transformed opacity into liability.
>
> — Ahmad Abbas, “Flag Theory 2.0: How Platforms Replaced Passports”

What replaces it, in his account, is the reverse of invisibility: "Multiple documented legal statuses across different sovereignty systems, each relationship fully transparent and compliant. You’re clearly present in several places, preventing any single government from exercising monopolistic control." He calls this "an individual-level separation of powers". The flags have also grown into platforms: one EU citizenship carries settlement rights across some thirty countries. Among his industry's clients, by 2025 "only one in four prioritized mobility"; the others cited tax-residency flexibility, asset protection, political insurance and family security. His summary of the goal: "You’re not escaping governments. You’re ensuring you’re never trapped by one."

Read this way, flag theory's lesson about mobility is about option value. Sovereignty comes from a credible ability to leave and a legal presence spread across several states, not from constant travel.

## Digital Flags

Recent writers add flags that are not countries at all. Phaneuf and Taghizadegan extend Schultz's list to money, publishing and communication:

> We might add to Schultz’s flag theory by emphasizing the importance of owning assets that are highly portable and outside the traditional banking system (Bitcoin, for example), hosting any websites on servers within countries that are committed to free speech, and communicating – whenever possible – over encrypted and decentralized means.
>
> — Emile Phaneuf III and Rahim Taghizadegan, “Jurisdiction Shopping: The Forgotten Logic Behind Flag Theory”

Katie The Russian goes further: "Cyberspace is the new flag, arguably the most important one, which takes away the necessity of many earlier flags." Abbas makes the same point about [[self-custody|self-custody]]: "One government can freeze your bank accounts, but not Bitcoin in cold storage." In this reading [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]] replaces much of the asset haven, and [[censorship-resistance|censorship-resistant]] hosting becomes a flag of its own.

Cyberspace still has a physical floor. [[julian-assange|Assange]] notes that the internet runs on undersea cables, satellites and "computer servers housed in buildings in cities from New York to Nairobi", and warns that the state "would soon learn to leverage its control of physical space to gain control over our platonic realm." A server is therefore itself a flag planted in some jurisdiction, and Phaneuf and Taghizadegan's advice accordingly names the hosting country, not only the software. A digital flag protects its owner only if it can be moved or replaced.

## Criticisms and Limits

- **Tax avoidance and civic duty.** Wikipedia's article presents the PT as someone hoping to "reduce taxes, avoid civic duties, and increase personal freedom", escaping obligations that include "income and asset taxes, social security contributions, jury duty, and military service". It records the description of the idea as a "late capitalist nomadism".
- **An industry's product.** According to the same article, PT books and services "have been a staple of companies that specialise in marketing offshore financial centres, tax avoidance schemes, and personal privacy services". Several of the modern sources sell what they describe: Katie The Russian runs Plan B Passport, and Abbas writes for a trade publication of the investment-migration business.
- **States respond.** Abbas argues that automatic information exchange ended the opacity the original model relied on, and reports that governments keep pushing back on the newer platforms: Washington suspended certain visa categories for Antigua and Barbuda and Dominica, and Brussels warned that citizenship-by-investment programs could lead to visa suspensions.
- **Exit for the few.** Starchild pitched the PT to "the upper minuscule of the population", and *The Sovereign Individual* predicts that "the information poor will either be tied by geography or will find little benefit from moving." This is the distributional objection developed in [[2026-07-02-bitcoin-frees-the-individual-not-the-collective|Bitcoin Frees the Individual, Not the Collective]]. Digital flags lower the cost of entry: holding keys and renting a server abroad needs no second passport.
