# Organized Crime and the Size of the Polity

> *Organized crime and the size of the polity is a public-choice and political-economy analysis of when a state can suppress the violent organizations operating inside its territory. Three converging results — the collective-action asymmetry between small concentrated interests and the large diffuse public, the identification of the state itself as the largest such concentrated organization of violence, and the rising cost of monitoring and force-concentration with population and territory — jointly imply that a small, centralized polity can carry out a sweeping crackdown far more cheaply than a large, federal, territorially vast one, and that the same capacity that lets it crush the gangs is the coercive monopoly that, on the other half of the same theory, makes the state the largest organized-crime operation of all.*

The subject is the mechanism, not a brief for or against any particular crackdown. It is the meeting point of [[public-choice-and-rational-ignorance|public choice]] on collective action, of [[charles-tilly|Charles Tilly]]'s historical sociology of the state as a protection racket, of [[murray-n-rothbard|Murray N. Rothbard]]'s libertarian definition of the state as the territorial monopolist of force, and of [[hans-hermann-hoppe|Hans-Hermann Hoppe]]'s political economy of decentralization and small polities. Each piece is well-established in the cited sources; the article's contribution is to align them along a single axis — polity size — and read off what they say together about a crackdown's feasibility and price.

## The Collective-Action Asymmetry

The first mechanism is the public-choice logic of collective action (associated with Mancur Olson) as summarized in [[william-f-shughart-ii|William F. Shughart II]]'s Concise Encyclopedia entry: a small group with high per-member stakes systematically out-organizes a large group whose interest is real but diffuse.

> "Small, homogeneous groups with strong communities of interest tend to be more effective suppliers of political pressure and political support (votes, campaign contributions, and the like) than larger groups whose interests are more diffuse. The members of smaller groups have greater individual stakes in favorable policy decisions, can organize at lower cost, and can more successfully control the free riding that otherwise would undermine the achievement of their collective goals."
>
> — [[william-f-shughart-ii|William F. Shughart II]], "Public Choice" (Concise Encyclopedia of Economics)

Shughart applies the result to legislatures: small organized interests (farmers, textile manufacturers) reliably extract subsidies and tariffs at the expense of the millions of unorganized consumers who pay the bill, and "representative democracy frequently leads to a tyranny of the minority." The structure is general. A gang, a mafia, a cartel is precisely the small, homogeneous, high-stakes group the logic describes; the population whose interest is not being preyed upon is precisely the diffuse, free-rider-prone many. Any one citizen's contribution to resistance has a small expected effect and a high private cost. A determined criminal minority can therefore dominate, intimidate, or extort a far larger population whose individual members each have weak private incentives to bear the cost of resistance.

By the same collective-action logic, advocacy is subject to the asymmetry too. A concentrated, motivated lobby — including one organized around the rights of the accused — can supply political pressure more cheaply than a large public whose interest in security is dispersed and lightly held by any one member. The logic predicts only which side can organize at lower cost, not which side is in the right; it does not, by itself, establish that any given advocacy leaves the public less safe.

## The State as the Competing — and Usually Winning — Concentrated Force

Crime suppression is not the diffuse majority defeating the concentrated minority. It is one concentrated organization of violence defeating another. On Tilly's reading, that asymmetry is built into what a state *is*. The opening of [[war-making-and-state-making-as-organized-crime|War Making and State Making as Organized Crime]] places states, as a matter of historical type and not metaphor, on the same continuum as criminal violence-wielders:

> "If protection rackets represent organised crime at its smoothest, then war making and state making – quintessential protection rackets with the advantage of legitimacy – qualify as our largest examples of organised crime."
>
> — [[charles-tilly|Charles Tilly]], [[war-making-and-state-making-as-organized-crime|War Making and State Making as Organized Crime]]

Tilly's continuum runs through "banditry, piracy, gangland rivalry, policing, and war making" as activities of the same kind, distinguished by scale, success, and the "advantage of legitimacy." Within it he identifies four interdependent activities by which states characteristically operate: "War making" (eliminating rivals outside the territory), "State making" (eliminating rivals inside the territory), "Protection" (eliminating "the enemies of their clients"), and "Extraction" (acquiring the means of doing the first three). All four "depend on the state's tendency to monopolies the concentrated means of coercion", and over time "the personnel of states purveyed violence on a larger scale, more effectively, more efficiently, with wider assent from their subject populations, and with readier collaboration from neighboring authorities than did the personnel of other organizations." On this account, fighting organized crime is the second of the four activities under a less embarrassing name — the state suppressing rivals inside the territory it already claims, exactly as the Tudors achieved what Lawrence Stone, quoted in Tilly, called "the ultimately successful assertion of a royal monopoly of violence both public and private".

Rothbard gives the same structural identification in libertarian terms. The opening of [[anatomy-of-the-state|Anatomy of the State]] defines the state by exactly the property a successful crackdown needs:

> "Briefly, the State is that organization in society which attempts to maintain a monopoly of the use of force and violence in a given territorial area; in particular, it is the only organization in society that obtains its revenue not by voluntary contribution or payment for services rendered but by coercion."
>
> — [[murray-n-rothbard|Murray N. Rothbard]], [[anatomy-of-the-state|Anatomy of the State]]

The state, in this account, has a direct institutional interest in suppressing rival predation — not because it values its subjects, but because predation is its preserve:

> "For the State, to preserve its own monopoly of predation, did indeed see to it that private and unsystematic crime was kept to a minimum; the State has always been jealous of its own preserve."
>
> — [[murray-n-rothbard|Murray N. Rothbard]], [[anatomy-of-the-state|Anatomy of the State]]

And the territorial form of that monopoly is exclusive: "Only one set of rulers can obtain a monopoly of coercion over any given territorial area at any one time". The Tillyan and Rothbardian definitions converge from non-libertarian and libertarian sides — see also the [[franz-oppenheimer|Oppenheimer]]/[[albert-jay-nock|Nock]] [[political-means-and-economic-means|political-means]] lineage on which Rothbard's definition rests — on the same operational claim. A successful crackdown on organized crime, in this shared descriptive vocabulary, is the larger and more legitimate monopoly of force eliminating a smaller and less legitimate one. A crackdown succeeds when the state actually exercises its territorial monopoly of force with enough will and capacity to displace those rivals.

## The Size of the Polity and the Cost of Monopolizing Force

If a crackdown is the state asserting its monopoly of force against rival violence-wielders, the cost of that assertion depends on the size and structure of the polity. The article infers that three cost margins tend to rise with population and territory.

- *Monitoring.* Surveilling a population and identifying the members of criminal organizations is cheaper when the territory is small, contiguous, and administratively direct. Tilly notes that "no European government approached the completeness of articulation from top to bottom achieved by imperial China" — government penetration of local life was costly, slow, and historically mediated by indirect rule through "junkers, justices of the peace, lords." The thicker the layer of indirect rule, the looser the central monopoly. Smaller, more centralized polities can largely dispense with it.
- *Coordination.* A federal structure fragments coercive authority across many jurisdictions, each with its own police, courts, and prisons; the central authority cannot impose a unified posture without overriding the sub-units in whose name federalism exists. A state's apparatus of internal surveillance and control is harder to unify across many subordinate jurisdictions than within one.
- *Force concentration.* Tilly cites economic-historical work (Lane, Bean) on the economies of scale in the production of effective force, counteracted by diseconomies of scale in command and control. A standing internal security force can be massed on a small territory more cheaply than on a vast one; on a vast one, the same nominal force is diluted across distance and against geographically entrenched, locally rooted criminal organizations.

These cost margins are the article's synthesis — an inference from Tilly's account of command-and-control diseconomies and the historical cost of penetrating local life, carried over to the size axis — rather than a separate documented finding about modern crime control.

The governance side of the same point is the [[jurisdictional-competition|inter-jurisdictional-competition]] argument in Hoppe's [[democracy-the-god-that-failed|Democracy: The God That Failed]]. Hoppe begins with the same Rothbardian premise:

> "A state is a territorial monopolist of compulsion—an agency which may engage in continual, institutionalized property rights violations and the exploitation—in the form of expropriation, taxation, and regulation—of private property owners."
>
> — [[hans-hermann-hoppe|Hans-Hermann Hoppe]], [[democracy-the-god-that-failed|Democracy: The God That Failed]]

His positive case is that smallness changes both a government's external incentives and its internal reach:

> "Smallness contributes to moderation, however. A small government has many close competitors, and if it taxes and regulates its own subjects visibly more than its competitors, it is bound to suffer from the emigration of labor and capital and a corresponding loss of future tax revenue."
>
> — [[hans-hermann-hoppe|Hans-Hermann Hoppe]], [[democracy-the-god-that-failed|Democracy: The God That Failed]]

Hoppe's own thrust is that political competition between small polities — culminating in his case for secession — is "a far more effective device for limiting a government's natural desire to expand its exploitative powers than are internal constitutional limitations", and that "the fact that Europe possessed a highly decentralized power structure composed of countless independent political units explains the origin of capitalism" in the West. The polity-size point for crime suppression runs his argument the other direction along the same axis. Hoppe's own claim is about exit and inter-jurisdictional competition; the application here is the article's inference, by analogy, that the factors making a small state cheaper to discipline from outside tend to be the same ones that make it cheaper to administer from within — a single chain of command, a population small enough to be saturated by police presence and identification, a territory small enough that little of it lies beyond the reach of the state's writ. The relevance here is the decentralization / small-polity side of Hoppe's argument, not the monarchy-versus-democracy thesis that runs alongside it in the same book.

On this reasoning, a large, federal, territorially vast state inverts each of those margins. It must coordinate enforcement across many independent jurisdictions; its criminal organizations are entrenched in geographically dispersed areas where local power-holders may collude with them or simply default; and the central authority cannot impose a unified posture without overriding the sub-units in whose name federalism exists. The same crackdown that is operationally feasible in a small, centralized polity is structurally far harder in a large, federal one — not because of any property of the criminals, but because the state's own monopoly is more expensive to concentrate and project at scale.

## The Libertarian Double-Edge

The fourth mechanism is the double-edge the analysis exposes. The very capacity that lets a state suppress the gangs is its monopoly of force, and that monopoly is the same one Tilly and Rothbard identify as the largest organized-violence operation in any given territory. A mass crackdown that overrides due process, sweeps a population into detention on suspicion, suspends rights of association, or co-opts the judiciary is the state exercising precisely the coercive monopoly Tilly places on the same continuum as "banditry, piracy, gangland rivalry, policing, and war making". Tilly's own closing line about the contemporary world is that "the analogy between war making and state making, on the one hand, and organized crime, on the other, is becoming tragically apt." On Rothbard's account the state is by definition the institution that obtains its revenue "by coercion" and "generally goes on to regulate and dictate the other actions of its individual subjects." The greater the will and capacity needed to crush organized crime within the territory, the greater the monopoly being exercised.

This is not a balancing argument that nets the two against each other. On the Tilly–Rothbard descriptive analysis there is no version of the state defeating the gangs that is not also the state exercising its territorial monopoly of force at full intensity; the two are the same act seen from two different normative angles. The asymmetry of section 1 is what makes the gangs possible; the monopoly of section 2 is what makes them suppressible; the polity-size logic of section 3 is what makes that monopoly cheaper or dearer to exercise; and the double-edge here is what they imply jointly. The honest description of a sweeping crackdown is therefore double — the same concentrated coercion that eliminates rival predation is the predation Tilly and Rothbard describe, now operating at the scale, with the legitimacy, and on the territory that distinguishes the state from any of its smaller competitors.

## Illustrative Cases (Mechanism, Not Empirical Claim)

The mechanisms above invite contrasts along the polity-size axis. Strictly as illustration of the mechanism — and not as empirical claims the cited sources establish — El Salvador under Bukele is widely cited in public debate as the contemporary instance of a small, centralized polity carrying out a sweeping crackdown, while Colombia and Brazil are frequently invoked as large, federal, territorially vast countries in which entrenched and geographically dispersed organized crime has proved far harder to suppress. The contrast functions only as a visual stand-in for the three structural mechanisms: a small centralized state mobilizing its monopoly of force against a relatively concentrated criminal sector on a small territory, versus large federal states whose authority is fragmented across jurisdictions and whose criminal organizations are entrenched in geographically distant regions. The substantive content of the analysis is the interaction of the collective-action asymmetry, the state's competing monopoly of force, the scaling of coercion costs with size, and the double-edge of the monopoly; the cases are useful only to ground the polity-size variable in something recognizable, and not for any judgment on the merits of any particular leader, government, or operation.

## Significance

The combined analysis explains why a recurring question — why can a government not simply suppress these gangs? — has structurally different answers in different polities, without recourse to claims about national character, leadership quality, or the criminals' particular wickedness. The *capacity* of a state to do the thing diffuse majorities most want it to do — eliminate predatory rivals to their persons and property — is, structurally, a small-polity capacity, and that very capacity is the largest version of the predation it suppresses. The same monopoly of force does both — eliminates the rival violence-wielders inside the territory, and constitutes the institution that, on Tilly's and Rothbard's accounts, is itself the largest organized-violence enterprise in that territory. The point matters because it cuts across the usual partisan framing: it is neither a triumphalist case for the strong-state crackdown nor a libertarian refusal to notice that crackdowns sometimes work. It describes how that joint condition obtains and at what structural cost; it does not, by itself, decide which half a reader should weight more heavily.
