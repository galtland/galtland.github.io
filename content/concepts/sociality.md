# Sociality

> Sociality is the single first principle on which [[samuel-pufendorf|Pufendorf]] grounds the interpersonal law of nature in [[the-whole-duty-of-man|The Whole Duty of Man According to the Law of Nature]] (1673). It is not a sentiment, not a love of one's kind, and not a duty of fusion into a collective. It is a hard, empirical claim about the human condition — that man is too weak to survive alone and too dangerous to be trusted unconditionally — together with the deductive rule that follows from it: that every person must so conduct himself toward his fellows that they have no cause to harm him. Read in its strong form, sociality can be read as the natural-law floor on which the libertarian moral order — non-injury, restitution, free cooperation among natural equals — rests.

## The Fundamental Law

Pufendorf's whole treatment of the duties men owe one another descends from a single sentence, stated in Book I, Chapter III as the foundation he has been building toward:

> "every man ought, as much as in him lies, to preserve and promote society."
>
> — [[samuel-pufendorf|Samuel Pufendorf]], [[the-whole-duty-of-man|The Whole Duty of Man According to the Law of Nature]] (Tooke trans.)

Two things make this load-bearing. First, Pufendorf labels it *fundamental*: every other interpersonal duty in Book I is derived from it as a consequence — the duties of natural equality, of not injuring, of keeping faith, of beneficence, of contract — so that an action necessary to the preservation of society is on that ground commanded, and one that would disturb or dissolve it is on that ground forbidden. Second, he glosses "society" immediately as the welfare of mankind, not as any particular state, tribe, or institution. The object of the duty is a distributed condition — peace and mutual benefit among individual persons — not a corporate body that could demand sacrifice in its own name.

Pufendorf is also careful to fence the principle. The duties of man toward God and toward himself are *not primarily or directly* derived from sociality: duties toward God may be indirectly deduced from it, and duties toward oneself arise jointly from religion and from the needs of society, but their immediate foundations lie elsewhere. Sociality governs, above all, what one person directly owes another.

## The Derivation: Weakness, Quarrel, and the Capacity to Harm

What makes Pufendorf's sociality more than a slogan is the way he gets to it. He builds it out of an honest description of the creature on whom the law is binding. Man, Pufendorf argues, is intensely desirous of his own preservation, beset by many wants, unable to support himself without the help of those of his kind, and yet well fitted in society to promote a common good. The same animal is also malicious, insolent, easily provoked, and no less prone to do mischief to his fellow than he is capable of effecting it.

The argument runs from these facts directly to the rule. Each person needs the help of others to survive, but cannot count on it, because others are equally needy, equally dangerous, and endlessly various in their dispositions. Man's proclivity to injure another, Pufendorf observes, is in fact stronger than in the brutes, which are usually content once hunger and lust are satisfied; man alone is driven by pride, envy, glory, and a desire to possess much more than he needs. And man is uniquely *capable* of harm — not by tooth and claw, but by the activity of his hands and the quickness of his wit, so that it is very easy for one man to bring upon another the greatest of natural evils, death itself.

Sociality is what survives this audit. The conclusion is forced, not sentimental: for his own preservation a man must be sociable — he must join with those of his kind and so behave toward them that they have no justifiable cause to do him harm, but rather to promote and secure his interests.

The form of this argument is what matters. Sociality is anthropologically motivated by each individual's interest in self-preservation, and then treated as binding law under Pufendorf's theistic account of obligation. The unit on which the deduction operates is the person, not the collective; the warrant is each person's need, not the group's claim. Society exists because individuals cannot survive alone, not the other way around. That asymmetry is the whole basis of the strong form.

## The Strong Form: Sociality as Nonaggression

The strong form of the argument is this: sociality, read strictly, can be read as an early natural-law ground for the later libertarian principle of [[nonaggression-and-property-rights|nonaggression]].

The first content of sociality is negative. Before Pufendorf gets to any positive duty of beneficence, he extracts the rule that each person must conduct himself so that others have no justifiable cause to do him harm. Read forward, this is the duty to give others no cause for legitimate complaint — the duty not to injure. Pufendorf states the categorical form bluntly in his treatment of damages: that no man is to be hurt or to receive damage; and because human nature is such that men would not forbear injuring one another, the same precept requires that whoever does cause harm make reparation. Non-injury and restitution fall out together as the minimum content of the law.

The fenced sphere this builds is recognizable. Pufendorf lists what the duty of not harming protects: what we have by the bounty of nature — life, body, limbs, chastity, liberty — together with whatever becomes our property by human institution or compact; so that those actions are made crimes by which a wrong is done to others, such as murder, wounding, striking, rapine, theft, fraud, and violence, whether done directly or indirectly. This is the catalog the libertarian tradition would later compress into a single principle. Pufendorf's sociality is what generates the catalog; the catalog is not bolted on from outside.

The *positive* duties Pufendorf later derives from sociality — beneficence, gratitude, the mutual good offices that are the product of sociality — are deliberately weaker: he distinguishes them as *imperfect* obligations, which one may fairly desire but cannot rightfully compel, from the *perfect* obligations of non-injury, restitution, and contract, which are enforceable as a matter of right. The strong form reads this distinction as decisive. What sociality requires *strictly* is the perfect duties: leave others' persons and property alone, repair the damage you cause, keep your word. What sociality requires only as imperfect moral duties — beneficence, kindness, brotherly love — it does not make enforceable as debts; they cannot be extracted by force without violating the very rule (no injury without justifiable cause) that the strict form sets down.

Sociality even underwrites a robust right of self-defense. Pufendorf holds that the sociality necessary to human life would become unworkable if a man could not resort even to extremities against one who irreclaimably persists in wronging him; otherwise honest men would be ready prey for villains. A man under attack is not required to forgo his own safety so an aggressor may practise his malice with impunity, and one who is hurt or slain in the course of his own aggression must impute the mischief to his own wickedness. On a libertarian reading, this is the inversion of any pacifist gloss: precisely because sociality is the basic law, force against aggressors is not a suspension of it but its enforcement.

## What Sociality Is Not: The Anti-Communitarian Hedge

The reading must be defended against an easy misreading. "Society" is exactly the word the modern collectivist tradition has used to override the individual — society as a corporate person with claims of its own. Pufendorf's sociality looks superficially like the same move, and it is sometimes glossed that way. The strong form denies the equivalence.

Three textual features cut against the collectivist reading. First, Pufendorf glosses "society" as the welfare of mankind — a distributed good across persons, not a claim possessed by any single body. Second, sociality is motivated through each individual's interest in self-preservation, and the duties he draws from it run person-to-person; the same chapter that grounds it also grounds a right of self-defense against anyone — which sits awkwardly with any demand that an individual be destroyed in the name of the whole. Third, the duties Pufendorf actually deduces from sociality — non-injury, contract, restitution, natural equality — are exhausted by the relations of person to person. There is no Pufendorfian "duty to society" that floats free of duties owed to identifiable other persons.

Pufendorf also writes natural equality into the same chapter on the duties owed to others: men are by nature equal, with none holding a natural rank or dominion over another, and no class of persons whose welfare counts for more. This is the structural barrier to the collectivist appropriation: sociality binds you to other natural equals one by one, not to an aggregate that can be represented by a sovereign or a majority against any one of them.

## Where Pufendorf Diverges From Libertarian Rights-Talk

The strong form is an appropriation, not a portrait of Pufendorf's own politics. Three differences are worth keeping clearly in view, lest sociality be confused with a Locke-before-Locke that it is not.

- **Duties before rights.** The whole architecture of *The Whole Duty of Man* is one of *officia* — duties of man toward God, self, and others. Subjective natural rights are present (notably in self-defense and property) but they are derivative from prior duties, not the moral primitive. A libertarian who reads Pufendorf for the sociality foundation should not also read him as a rights-first theorist.
- **Religion as social cement.** Pufendorf holds that man could not become a sociable creature were he not imbued with religion, and that reason in religion reaches only so far as it is useful to promote the common tranquillity and sociality of this life. That instrumental role for piety is not what either a Christian natural-law theorist or a secular rights theorist would say; it is its own position, and it makes sociality a more demanding moral atmosphere than a thin non-aggression rule.
- **Sovereignty as the cure for human malice.** The same anthropology that gives us sociality gives Pufendorf, in Book II, the argument for strong civil power. The vast diversity of men's dispositions, and their proneness to mischief, is read as a standing reason for a sovereign able to enforce the law of nature among them. A libertarian appropriation of sociality has to do its own work to show that voluntary law and decentralized enforcement can carry that weight.

The conservative summary is that sociality is a clean foundation for the interpersonal law of nature and a generous bridge to libertarian ethics, but Pufendorf builds a different house on it than rights-first libertarianism would. The principle is portable; the building is his.

## Place in the Natural-Law Lineage

In this wiki's reading of the [[natural-law-and-natural-order|natural-law]] lineage, sociality is what Pufendorf contributes between [[hugo-grotius|Grotius]] and [[john-locke|Locke]]. Grotius had detached natural law from theology enough to ground a law of nations (the *etiamsi daremus*) but had left its foundations diffuse; Pufendorf supplies a single principle, derived from observable human nature, deductively rich enough to generate the rest of the interpersonal law of nature. Locke, who read Pufendorf, works in the same idiom — men bound by the law of nature to abstain from injuring one another in life, liberty, or possessions — and sharpens it into the explicit equal-natural-rights theory of the [[the-second-treatise-of-government|Second Treatise]] that the modern liberal and libertarian traditions inherit. (The Grotius and Locke claims here are the wiki's placement of Pufendorf, developed in those articles; this page is sourced only to Pufendorf's own text.)

Sociality is also the move that helps make natural law *portable* across the confessional divides opened by the wars of religion: it grounds the law of nature in features of the human condition that any honest observer can recognize, rather than in a contested metaphysics. That portability is part of why a sociality-derived nonaggression principle remains a serviceable foundation today.
