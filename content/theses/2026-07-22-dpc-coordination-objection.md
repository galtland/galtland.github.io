# The Coordination Objection: A Reply to an Ex-Libertarian

> The most instructive critique of libertarianism in this corpus comes not from a hostile outsider but from a developer who knows the arguments, builds the tools, and left anyway. dpc's three objections — that society binds harder than the state, that capitalism optimizes productivity rather than flourishing, that power re-consolidates whatever the design — land almost entirely on ground this corpus already concedes in its own voice. Traced across four years, his trajectory is not an exit from the tradition but an orbit around its one unbuilt piece: a voluntary, binding, meaning-bearing layer of coordination, which he models on halal and kosher observance and the adoption-problem thesis independently names as the unifying meaning the movement has never produced. His coordination-kernel government is a minarchist layer if its scope stays at adjudication and protection; his competing-gangs argument is jurisdictional competition. The reply he is owed is therefore not a refutation of his diagnosis but a demonstration that his remedy converges on the tradition's own unbuilt piece — and that his single departure from his own logic, the June 2026 turn toward a higher-level holistic economic optimizer, is the one position his irreducible-power premise forbids him to hold, because a central optimizer is the richest prize available for exactly the capture he says is inevitable.

## The interlocutor

Dawid Ciężarkiewicz, who writes as dpc (@dpc_pw), is a working Rust developer in [[bitcoin|Bitcoin]] and Fedimint — federated [[ecash|ecash]] — infrastructure. He knows the arguments and left anyway, which sets the register for everything below: his departure cannot be answered by introducing him to arguments he already holds. His objection is not that the tradition lacks an account of property, prices, dispersed knowledge, or aggression; it is that those accounts do not solve the coordination problems that actually bind individuals. In September 2022 he declared the exit (the spelling in all quotations here is his own):

> I'm an ex-libertarian. I eventually understood and accepted some things: 1) Social and economical pressures are far, far more dangerous and limiting to individual freedom than direct "violence". Humans are social animals and can't survive on their own.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 12 Sep 2022

The same thread continues:

> Group will always impose its will overpowering the individual. Government is just a formalization of that. You maybe can get rid of government, but you can't get rid of the group. 4) Markets and individual choice are far more stupid and hopeless than what libertarians believe.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 12 Sep 2022

And, the same day, the residual allegiance that makes the case worth answering rather than filing — the available extract contains no later retraction:

> Note: I was (and am still strongly leaning) libertarian right ancap.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 12 Sep 2022

The cited extract draws on a 3,769-post archive spanning April 2022 to July 2026, of which 194 posts are ideological; the feed is overwhelmingly Rust, Nix, and Bitcoin engineering. Two limits bound everything below. Roughly a quarter of the account's posts are missing from the mirrors, so the absence of a view is weak evidence of anything; and the ideological posts are the asides of a working engineer, evidence of a trajectory rather than a worked-out program. This reply reads them as such — a first-person account of exit from anarcho-capitalism by someone fluent in the canon, which is exactly what makes it a rare test case — and holds its conclusions with the modesty owed to a position reconstructed from asides.

## What should be conceded first

Nothing below is argued from a fortress. [[objections-to-libertarianism|Objections to Libertarianism]] names the empirical adoption and stability of a free society as one of the four points where the corpus's answers are weakest or most genuinely contested. [[criticisms-of-anarcho-capitalism|Criticisms of Anarcho-Capitalism]] rates its own confidence medium, holds [[robert-nozick|Nozick]]'s invisible-hand argument — that a dominant protective agency, and with it a minimal state, would legitimately re-emerge from anarchy — genuinely unresolved, and concedes that no institutional design is self-enforcing: the durability of any order, a constitutional minimal state included, rests on culture and ideas more than on formal structure. Anarcho-capitalism can describe how competing protection agencies might prefer arbitration to war; it cannot demonstrate from historical evidence that they would remain competitive, scale to collective defense, resist cartelization, or prevent a dominant agency from becoming a state. So when dpc presses on stability, consolidation, and adoption, he is pressing where the tradition, in its honest moments, already gives way. The task of a reply is not to show he has missed something but to establish what his objections do and do not license.

## First claim: society binds harder than the state

*Stated at full strength: a society can respect every formal property title while coordinating so thoroughly against a minority that the minority loses meaningful social and economic agency. If libertarianism calls that society free because no one initiates violence, it has mistaken one boundary of justice for the whole of freedom — the non-aggression principle guards the wrong perimeter.*

His test case, put to libertarians as a direct question:

> Is a cast system in which super-majority of population forms a socially enforced consensus on treating an arbitrary minority like shit, denying them social and economic opportunities OK with libertarians, as long as NAP is not vilated?
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 12 Sep 2022

The question is built as a reductio, and the honest answer is to bite it. Under [[nonaggression-and-property-rights|nonaggression and property rights]], yes: a caste order enforced purely through shunning, refusal to deal, and social consensus violates no one's rights, in the specific sense that no one in it may be stopped by force. The canon's most systematic treatment of exactly this terrain — [[hans-hermann-hoppe|Hoppe]]'s account of [[forced-integration|forced integration]], stated most fully in [[democracy-the-god-that-failed|Democracy: The God That Failed]] — does not deny the possibility. It treats state-mandated association as the aggression and answers it with voluntary separation, and Hoppe accepts the illiberal consequence without flinching: covenant communities and towns with entrance requirements, exclusion on whatever criteria their members set, religion and ethnicity explicitly contemplated. Softening this would misrepresent the tradition. Its hardest-edged theorist replies to the caste case not by denying it could happen but by defending the right of voluntary communities to run exactly such exclusions — a bullet bitten, not dodged.

Where the objection is strongest is not in what the rule permits but in what the rule measures. [[hannah-arendt|Arendt]]'s distinction in [[on-violence|On Violence]] between [[violence-vs-power|violence and power]] substantially concedes his premise from outside the tradition: power is what people have when they act in concert, violence is instrumental and depends on tools, and violence can destroy power but never create it. On that vocabulary, a supermajority's capacity to ruin someone without laying a finger on him is power in the primary sense, and the state is one formalization of it — which is very close to his own sentence about government. A rule indexed to violence is indexed to the derivative variable, exactly as he says. The concession costs less than it looks, because it is about scope, not truth: a rule about when force may be used is not a theory of everything that can go badly wrong in a human life, and no one in the canon claimed otherwise — though anyone who turns that distinction into the claim that nonviolent exclusion is harmless has left the argument behind. But it does mean the answer that the NAP is not violated is a jurisdictional reply, not a moral one, and he is right to find it thin.

What the concession does not establish is that a higher sovereign is the cure — and here his own words close the escape route. "Government is just a formalization of that." he writes, of the group's will overpowering the individual. Grant it. Then in his caste case, a state empowered to break the caste is the formalization of the very supermajority whose "socially enforced consensus" constitutes it. An enforcer strong enough to police social and economic pressure across a whole society is not a neutral third party standing outside the group; on his own account of what government is, it is the group's instrument — available as the majority's instrument, and so no automatic cure. The NAP guards the violence perimeter not because its theorists judged social pressure harmless, but because that is the one line where the guard does not become the thing guarded against.

His anthropological premise, meanwhile, is the tradition's own starting point rather than a discovery deployed against it. That humans cannot survive alone is precisely where [[samuel-pufendorf|Pufendorf]] begins in [[sociality|sociality]], worked out in [[the-whole-duty-of-man|The Whole Duty of Man]]: man is too needy to live without his own kind and too dangerous to be trusted unconditionally, and the whole interpersonal law of nature is deduced from that pair. The direction of the deduction is the thing to notice — the unit is the person, and society exists because individuals cannot survive alone, not the reverse. dpc runs the same premise the opposite way, from dependence to the group's standing authority; that directional choice, not the sociality premise both sides share, is the real disagreement, and the shared premise settles it for neither side. What follows for Pufendorf is a graded structure: a hard, enforceable floor of non-injury, reparation, and contract, and above it a real but non-compellable duty of humanity — good offices whose refusal is fairly charged as unkindness but which cannot be exacted by force. The caste boycott sits in the upper register. That classification is not a dodge; it is what keeps beneficence from becoming a claim enforceable at gunpoint, and it concedes his substance in the only way that matters: the tradition treats his case as a genuine wrong that no one may be compelled to remedy. One caveat travels with this: Pufendorf himself proceeds from sociality to a civil sovereign charged with common security, so his account broadens libertarian moral anthropology without by itself vindicating anarcho-capitalist institutions.

What remains is the practical question of what erodes a caste consensus, and the tradition's answer is narrower than he wants but not empty: competition, persuasion, exit, and the standing cost that [[milton-friedman|Friedman]] identifies in [[capitalism-and-freedom|Capitalism and Freedom]] — the man who discriminates pays for it, in a market where his rivals will hire and serve whomever he refuses. Friedman opposes compulsion in both directions and reaches for competition from the same premise Hoppe uses to reach separation, which is a fair measure of how much room the premise leaves. There is also the mechanism dpc himself later proposes as the answer to coordination failure: intolerant minorities can organize against a caste consensus as well as for one. Whether these instruments are sufficient against a determined supermajority is an empirical question the corpus does not settle. The reply is therefore narrower than a victory: the non-aggression principle remains indispensable as a rule governing enforceable claims and insufficient as a complete theory of social freedom. He is entitled to find that answer unsatisfying; he is not free to skip the question of who enforces the better one.

## Second claim: the wrong objective function

*Stated at full strength: market competition is not neutral coordination but a selection process — a cybernetic system that rewards whatever captures revenue and survives, productivity and captured value rather than leisure, dignity, community, or flourishing. A system optimizing the wrong objective cannot be repaired by running it more consistently; it needs a higher-level coordinator.*

His earliest formulation predates the exit:

> There's no escaping the fact that capitalism is just a cybernetic system that maximizes productivity growth. Not "humanness", free time, well being or anything else. Only productivity.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 30 Jul 2022

By 2024 the complaint had sharpened into resignation — and it is a builder's post, not a critic's, its verdict on the available alternatives more favorable to markets than most defenders bother to be:

> For humanity to progress, we need to organize using tools of cybernetics, distributed systems, complexity theory, game theory, etc. serving our philosophical beliefs and goals. Yet there's nothing remotely like that on a political horizon anywhere. We're stuck in place where liberal capitalism is the best we can do as for all its flaws it at least harnesses competitive forces. It's depressing, when you see we're doomed to be stuck.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 14 Jul 2024

By 2026 it had compressed into aphorism — "Capitalism is a good servant, but bad master." — and the sharper charge that "capitalism is about the value you can capture, and not the value you create.".

Concede the capture point first, because the tradition already makes it at length. The corpus's name for returns that flow to political position rather than production is [[crony-capitalism|crony capitalism]]: [[frederic-bastiat|Bastiat]]'s legal plunder, [[murray-n-rothbard|Rothbard]]'s grants of monopoly privilege, and the [[public-choice-and-rational-ignorance|public-choice]] logic of concentrated benefits and dispersed costs, which makes rent-seeking a standing tendency rather than an occasional abuse. The libertarian move is to say this is the [[political-means-and-economic-means|political means]] wearing a market mask and therefore not capitalism. He would reply that a distinction unenforceable in practice is not much of a distinction — and the corpus's own article treats the enforceability of that distinction as contested: whether market profit and political privilege can be kept institutionally separate when wealth can purchase influence and government can sell it is exactly the unresolved question. Applied to the Western hybrid that actually exists — the system his February 2026 post describes as "Government overtaken by capital" — his capture line is not an objection to the tradition's account. It is the tradition's account. The dispute here is over the name of the phenomenon, not its existence or its size, and being clear about that removes an argument he would otherwise have to keep making.

The optimizer frame is where the disagreement actually lives. To say capitalism "maximizes productivity growth" is to attribute to the market a global objective function, and the claim of [[friedrich-a-hayek|Hayek]]'s [[spontaneous-order|spontaneous order]] and the [[knowledge-problem|knowledge problem]] is not that the market optimizes the right objective but that it has no single, centrally specified objective function. A cybernetic optimizer has an objective function because someone specifies one; a market has no place where a global objective is represented — only the differing ends of millions of people, coordinated through prices, drawing on knowledge that exists nowhere in aggregate. What competition selects for is not productivity as such but whatever buyers actually pay for — including free time, quiet, and craft, to the exact extent people will part with money for them. Productivity growth is a by-product of mutual adjustment, not a target anyone set, which is why no one can be found who set it. That is a deliberately weaker claim than the claim that capitalism optimizes flourishing: the system is silent about the ranking of ends rather than adversarial to it. Silence is a real limitation and worth resenting. It is not the same defect as optimizing the wrong thing — and the difference matters, because only the second admits a fix of the kind he wants.

The cybernetic idiom itself is not the dispute: on the Hayekian reading, the price system is the feedback channel. The dispute is whether a higher loop can be closed above it. That would require human flourishing to be statable as an objective function at all — an aggregation over plural, often incompatible ends that this tradition denies exists anywhere as a fact to be known; supplied with knowledge that is local, tacit, perishable, and partly created by competition itself; and able to evaluate trade-offs without the exchange-generated prices it overrides. Hayek's point is not that coordinators are unintelligent or malicious; it is that the counterfactual knowledge needed to compare unrealized plans never exists in one place.

None of this proves that emergent outcomes are good. Spontaneous order expressly does not mean every undesigned pattern is just, efficient, or humane; markets depend on a designed framework of property and law; and where costs land on people who never agreed and bargaining is impractical, the coordination really is broken — the corpus lists global, diffuse externalities among the points where its own answers are thinnest. His objective-function critique therefore establishes a design requirement — market institutions need moral, legal, and cultural constraints, and some valuable things will remain outside prices — not a case for comprehensive direction. Whether a comprehensive corrector could do better than what it overrides is the question his June 2026 post finally answers in the affirmative, and that answer gets its own section below, because it is where his position stops being a hard case for libertarianism and becomes a hard case for itself.

## Third claim: power always comes back

*Stated at full strength: every arrangement contains decisions about rules, enforcement, admission, and change. Those decisions create power; organization concentrates it; sooner or later someone captures it. Anarcho-capitalism can rename the institutions, but it cannot remove the underlying potential — which tends to re-consolidate, and that no design has yet escaped it.*

The mature statement came in July 2026:

> "Real anarcho-capitalism have never been tried."
>
> The libertarians have instincts right, but refuse to accept/realize that potential of power is just there, irreducible, and someone sooner or later is going to consolidate it.
>
> Barely ever can have HoA or a chess club without power drama, and somehow we are supposed to have whole societies...
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 15 Jul 2026

It had been building since 2023:

> In practice 99% of the public has no idea what's going on and delegates power to a combination of aristocrats (developers) and oligarchs (businesses). Bitcoin is not exempt from the Iron Law of Oligarchy.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 30 Jan 2023

And in its most compressed form: "The question of power still remains: someone has to get to change (or not) the rules."

This is the objection that lands on the corpus's weakest ground, and the concessions above already grant most of it: no institutional design is self-enforcing, durability rests on culture and ideas more than structure, and Nozick's re-emergence argument is unresolved. His HoA-and-chess-club line is a compressed statement of the stability objection the corpus itself takes most seriously. It is the strongest of his three claims, and it is conceded.

What is contestable is the word *regardless*. [[megapolitics|Megapolitics]] — the frame of [[the-sovereign-individual|The Sovereign Individual]] — and the [[offense-defense-balance-of-technology|offense–defense balance of technology]] treat the returns to violence, and therefore the returns to consolidation, as a technological variable rather than a constant: when the cost of projecting power falls, coercion concentrates and small polities are absorbed; when it rises, predation stops paying and power scatters. On that reading the Iron Law is not repealed — someone still consolidates — but the scale at which consolidation pays is set by technology, and moving that scale is an engineering problem rather than a moral appeal. If the potential of power were simply "just there, irreducible", the historical variety of political forms would need some other explanation. This is a wager, not a law — the corpus rates those pages low and medium confidence respectively — but it is also the working premise of his own career. His fullest statement of what he actually believes:

> Personal choice doesn't work collectively. [...] I am freedom loving, but at some point one needs to realize that voluntarily* restricting one's collective short-term freedom is beneficial for one's individual freedom in the long-term.
>
> Bitcoin works precisely because all its users agree to follow restrictive consensus rules. [...] World is full of coordination traps. Unless humans realize they need to navigate these balancing short term and long term, individual and collective, freedom and responsibility, we're doomed.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 26 Sep 2024

The important word is *voluntarily*. Bitcoin does not eliminate protocol power; it makes rules explicit, verification broadly available, and continued participation conditional on acceptance — a particular distribution of power and a particular cost structure for changing the rules, which is a partial and real answer to "someone has to get to change (or not) the rules." [[self-custody|Self-custody]] and federated custody are bets that the cost of taking a thing from a person can be pushed up far enough to change who bothers; [[proof-of-work|proof of work]] is the canonical instance of attaching a physical cost to action in a domain that had none. His Iron Law post says Bitcoin is not exempt — granted, and this corpus claims exemption for no one. But *not exempt* and *unaffected* are different claims. The design did not abolish the gradient; it moved the price of climbing it. A man who spends his working life on consensus rules built to make rule-change expensive, and on federated custody built to make trust divisible, is not in practice treating the potential for power as beyond design. Power cannot be abolished, but its concentration, scope, contestability, and returns can be changed — weaker than the anarchist promise of eliminating rule, if anyone made that promise, and stronger than the fatalist conclusion that every design converges on the same sovereign.

## The convergence: his remedy has the shape of the tradition's open problem

The pivotal post is from May 2024, and it is the reason this article exists:

> The only solution to Moloh is "religious fundamentalism" like coordination. The reason why software keeps enslaving is that Freedom preserving software can't compete (mostly economically) with larger economy of slave software.
>
> If we were able to create religion like group that one can join to gain benefits, but must respect in group rules, we could win. Think "halal" or "cosher" food, religious fundamentalism. Google Taleb's "the most intolerant win".
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 10 May 2024

Read it slowly. The remedy for Moloch — his shorthand for the coordination trap in which freedom-preserving options lose to better-funded unfree ones — is a group joined voluntarily for its benefits, whose in-group rules genuinely bind. The post specifies neither how those rules are enforced nor whether members may leave; what it invokes is the minority dynamic Taleb names: a disciplined community whose non-negotiable rules propagate, on the model of halal and kosher observance, because deviation costs more than compliance. That is a design, not a mood.

Now set beside it what this corpus said independently, from entirely different sources, in [[2026-06-08-libertarianism-and-the-adoption-problem|Libertarianism and Human Nature: The Adoption Problem]]: that the classic routes to a freer order are each necessary and none sufficient, that what mobilizes people is belonging rather than argument, and that the one thing the movement has never built is a unifying meaning capable of supplying the belonging [[nationalism-and-state-formation|nationalism]] supplies — a gap the thesis traces to the [[in-group-out-group-tribalism|in-group/out-group]] sentiment that costs nationalism nothing to mobilize. These are the same shape at different resolutions: his describes the institutional rules, the thesis describes the belonging that makes people willing to follow them. His is the more operational, because it specifies the mechanism — benefits at the door, binding rules inside, an intolerant minority setting the standard. It even has a precedent inside the tradition he left: Hoppe's covenant communities are a close precedent with the same exclusion-based enforcement — though territorial and proprietary where his model is non-territorial observance.

Note the structural symmetry with his own caste case, because it is load-bearing. The halal/kosher device is a socially enforced consensus that binds individuals without violating property — precisely the mechanism his 2022 question presented as libertarianism's embarrassment. His caste case and his halal case share a mechanism — socially enforced norms that bind without anyone committing aggression. They are not the same object: they differ in purpose, in heredity, in what happens to those outside. But consent and exit at the boundary are the safeguards his own proposal implicitly leans on, and they are exactly what the caste case lacks. The line between the mechanism he fears and the mechanism he prescribes is exactly the line the NAP polices. The perimeter he called wrongly drawn in 2022 turns out to be load-bearing for the remedy he proposed in 2024 — and it is why [[secession|secession]] and [[seasteading-and-network-states|network states]] sit where they do in this corpus: not as escapism but as the institutional form of the one thing that makes binding in-group rules tolerable. Nor does the underlying position depart from the property-and-contract spine — though not by way of contract. The [[title-transfer-theory-of-contract|title-transfer theory of contract]] holds that a bare promise transfers no title and is therefore unenforceable; only alienable property can be transferred. In-group rules are accordingly not binding contracts but associational conditions, enforced by exclusion — withholding the benefits that brought members to the door. That is an ordinary exercise of the owner's right to exclude, which is the same right [[forced-integration|forced integration]] is about, and it is the material [[market-anarchism-and-private-law|private-law order]] is made of. What the position he left lacks is not permission but the artifact. Nobody has built the thing, and saying so is the honest description of where the argument stands.

His other recurring formulations converge the same way. The one that reappears across the archive nearly verbatim:

> What libertarians don't get is that there is an obvious role for "the government", and it's the same as for OS kernel - coordination.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 11 Sep 2022

A minimal layer legitimated by function — scheduling and interfaces, not sanctity — is [[minarchism|minarchism]]: the night-watchman state argued from systems architecture instead of natural rights, defended by people who share his distrust of the thing they are defending. The metaphor does not determine the kernel's scope, which is why the June 2026 turn matters — a minimal adjudicative framework and a comprehensive economic scheduler are radically different architectures. One month after the exit declaration, he had also already stated the tradition's other answer to consolidation from scratch:

> If you are a Bitcoiner you need to understand: we're all trapped by game theory: we have to support a terrible lying violent gang to protect us from other violent gangs. And having one global gang would be even bigger disaster because it would eliminate the competition
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 16 Oct 2022

That is [[jurisdictional-competition|jurisdictional competition]] in its harshest vocabulary: the answer to irreducible power is not its abolition but its division, so that exit disciplines the price. He will need that argument again below. And his cultural conclusion, from the end of 2024:

> The ideal human civilization is something like space-faring Amish. What Amish get right is avoiding the trap of unconstrained modernism, liberalism and technology being self-destructive for human society. What they get wrong is rejecting useful aspects of it.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 31 Dec 2024

A voluntary, rule-bound, technologically selective community that refuses modernity's self-destructive defaults while keeping its useful parts — the same shape a third time. None of this denies his chosen label. He has left libertarianism as a declared identity and rejects the sufficiency of several of its central answers. But his remedies — voluntary rule-bound association, competitive jurisdictions, protocol constraints, a common coordination layer — sit inside the tradition's unresolved institutional frontier. He is not returning to a problem libertarianism solved. He is working on the problem it has not solved.

## Where the position breaks: the holistic optimizer

In June 2026 the blank left open in the 2024 posts gets filled in, and this is the one position that cannot survive contact with his own commitments:

> In The Goal, it's well explained how the cardinal sin of system optimization is optimizing each part in separation. Capitalism is basically each part of the economy optimizing not only in separation but adversarialy to the whole...
>
> In Thermoeconomics in Time of Monsters, it's well explained how Chinese economic model is a combination of bottom level capitalism with higher-level holistic economic system optimization, all contrasted with western economic model.
>
> Quite clear to me that it's a superior way to think about economics, where western squables of naive capitalism with naive socialism now seem like backwater superstitions.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 26 Jun 2026

The systems insight is sound at the level of an engineered organization: optimizing each component's local metric can degrade a designed whole. The inference to an economy does not follow, because a factory has an owner, a defined output, an accounting boundary, and a purpose supplied from outside the system, while a society contains people with different purposes and no uncontested scalar measure of the whole.

The Austrian objection has to be scoped honestly or it will be dismissed, correctly, as a slogan. [[ludwig-von-mises|Mises]]'s [[economic-calculation-problem|calculation problem]] is an in-principle result about full socialization: without private ownership and exchange of capital goods there are no money prices for them, no common unit in which the countless technically possible production plans can be compared, and rational calculation is impossible rather than merely hard. A hybrid that keeps bottom-level capitalism keeps its prices, so the argument does not simply flatten the model; it presents a dilemma instead. To the extent the higher level overrides ownership, investment, and capital allocation comprehensively, it destroys the price formation on which comparison depends; to the extent it defers to bottom-level prices, it is not holistic — it is an interventionist participant using partial market signals while imposing selected political priorities, and the objection bites at each margin where an override displaces a price, harder the more the optimizer optimizes. The knowledge problem bites everywhere, because the optimizer's difficulty is not arithmetic but epistemic: the knowledge prices summarize is local, tacit, perishable, and never available in one place, and a holistic objective function presupposes a ranking of ends that exists nowhere as a fact to be known. Mises constrains the strong claim that a non-market center can calculate the whole; Hayek constrains the fallback claim that preserving prices gives the center the contextual knowledge to improve on them. Neither argument is a proof about any particular economy's growth statistics. Both are reasons to expect the higher level to be systematically worse informed than what it overrides.

The damaging objection, though, is not Austrian. It is his own, and he supplies it himself four months earlier:

> As a very pro free markets and personal freedom loving person, I think it is a good time to take an L and acknowledge that just like communism in practice degenerates into totalitarian dystopia, free markets / capitalism degenerates into ... this ... what the West has right now. Government overtaken by capital, masses hypnotized by propaganda, secret societies worshiping Moloch, military industrial complex killing millions, social fabric dismantled.
>
> You might laugh at "real communism have never been tried", and then look at all that is happening and say... "acktually, this is not a real capitalism"? Basically "real capitalism have never been tried"?
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 11 Feb 2026

"Government overtaken by capital" is a capture diagnosis, delivered about a far less centralized arrangement than the one he now recommends. If the potential for power is irreducible and someone will eventually consolidate it — his own sentence, nineteen days after the Chinese-model post — then a centralized authority empowered to set the economy's higher-level objectives is the richest capture target on offer: a single control point whose capture pays off across the entire economy at once, joining rule-making, capital direction, information, and enforcement in the institution most worth controlling. Firms that seek captured rather than created value — his own description of the present — would rationally redirect their efforts toward capturing it. If the Iron Law reaches Bitcoin's developers, the ministry of holistic optimization does not stay clean either; and unlike the chess club, its drama binds everyone.

His February post also supplies the discipline that should govern the comparison. He refuses to let capitalism be judged by its brochure — "real capitalism have never been tried" is, on his telling, a laugh line of the same species as its communist twin, and by July 2026 he extends the same mockery to "Real anarcho-capitalism have never been tried." Consistency asks that the third system face the same test. Judged by description, the Chinese model is bottom-level markets under benevolent holistic correction; judged by the record his own premise predicts, it is the largest concentration of the thing he says always gets consolidated. His own line closes the loop: "The question of power still remains: someone has to get to change (or not) the rules." Someone has to hold the objective function. He can hold that power always re-consolidates, or he can endorse the thing most worth consolidating; holding both requires an account of why this particular concentration is the one that stays clean, and no post in the corpus supplies it. Either institutions can alter the consolidation and capture of power — in which case design, competition, exit, and technological defense remain meaningful — or consolidation is fated, in which case concentrating economic coordination only strengthens the eventual captor. A central optimizer cannot be exempted from the power theory he applies to every decentralized alternative — and everything else in the archive, from the competing-gangs post to the Iron Law post to a working life spent making rule-change expensive, says which premise he actually believes.

## The record is not monotonic

A fair reading quotes the posts that cut the other way, because the drift is not a straight line. As late as September 2023, a year after the exit, he was defending capitalism against its lazier critics:

> You know who never complains about capitalism? People who experienced the lack of it. They will risk their lives to get somewhere with capitalism and they will be happy if they made it for the rest of their lives.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 30 Sep 2023

The competing-gangs post of October 2022 — one month after the exit declaration — is as libertarian an argument as the archive contains. And when he does break explicitly with a libertarian position, in November 2024 on usury, the shape of the break is telling:

> The naive libertarian could say: "that's price control". The pragmatist understands that usury is evil, and these pray on people's stupidity and desperation. Unproductive debt is a soft form of slavery, and basic dignity should make us not let fellow citizens ruin their lives like that.
>
> — Dawid Ciężarkiewicz (@dpc_pw), *X Corpus, Ideological Extract (2022–2026)*, 10 Nov 2024

The self-description is "pragmatist", not convert; the ground is "basic dignity", not a rival system; and nothing general about markets follows from it. (The extract does not preserve the anti-redistribution post sometimes attributed to the same month, so no weight is placed on that juxtaposition here.) The December 2024 conclusion is cultural rather than economic — the space-faring Amish, keeping the constraint and dropping the rejection of useful technology. The pattern across four years is not a straight line away from anything. It is someone holding a market prior and a coordination prior at the same time and being visibly unable to reconcile them — which is not confusion but an accurate report of the state of the problem, until the single 2026 step onto ground his own premises foreclose.

## What remains contested

Nothing here settles the disagreement, and several pieces of it cannot be settled from this corpus at all.

- **Whether the non-coercive remedies are sufficient.** That competition, persuasion, and exit erode a socially enforced caste order is a prediction, not a demonstration, and Hoppe's version of the same claim — that separation yields cooperative cultural selection — is asserted more than shown. Voluntary separation shows a NAP-consistent answer exists; it does not show that a society of exclusive covenants would be good to live in, and Hoppe's acceptance of the illiberal consequence will strike many readers as conceding dpc's point in different words. The gap between a rights-answer and a flourishing-answer is real, and it is the gap his objection names.
- **Whether the state comes back anyway.** Nozick's invisible-hand argument is unresolved on its merits, and the stability objection is conceded in principle: no institutional design is self-enforcing, a constitutional minimal state included — which historically grows rather than staying minimal. If that concession is fatal, it is fatal to minarchism and to the holistic optimizer as well; but it is fatal to anarcho-capitalism first, and nothing in this reply repairs the corpus's named weakness on empirical adoption and stability.
- **Whether the cost asymmetry holds.** The claim that technology can raise the returns to defense enough to change the scale of consolidation is a wager backed by the operational survival of a few systems, defeatable by physical coercion, key mistakes, or centralization — and the same networks that enable verification and exit can enable surveillance and control. The corpus itself rates megapolitics low confidence and the offense–defense balance medium. The [[parallel-economy|parallel-economy]] strategy and the argument of [[2026-07-02-bitcoin-frees-the-individual-not-the-collective|Bitcoin Frees the Individual, Not the Collective]] both rest on it, and both are honest that it is a wager.
- **Whether value capture is corrigible.** The libertarian fix is to shrink the privileges available for purchase; the standing objection is that capture is endogenous to concentrated wealth and that the distinction between market profit and political favor is unenforceable in practice. The corpus does not resolve this, and his version of the complaint is the strong one.
- **Whether a voluntary creed can bind without curdling.** A group strong enough to overcome coordination traps must be able to discipline defectors, and its benefits may make exit formally voluntary but materially costly. The proposal needs a theory of dissent, expulsion, rule change, competing communities, and limits on accumulated authority; without those, religion-like coordination can become a socially enforced caste in another form. His halal/kosher proposal and the corpus's unbuilt-creed problem are one question, and no one — not he, not the movement — has answered it by building the thing.
- **The evidentiary base is thin by construction.** 194 ideological asides from a feed that is overwhelmingly engineering, with roughly a quarter of the account unrecoverable. A trajectory reconstructed from asides is not a system, and this reply's confidence is bounded accordingly.

What can be said plainly is narrower, and it is the article's finding. His first and third claims are conceded here in the terms he set them. The second is conceded in its rent-seeking half and answered in its cybernetic half, where the answer stands or falls on whether a system with no place to represent a global objective can be said to be optimizing the wrong one. The single position that fails on his own premises is the holistic optimizer — not because Mises said so, but because the man who says power always re-consolidates has endorsed the most concentrated lever on offer. Everything else he has argued since 2022 sits inside the frontier of the position he says he left: coordination-kernel government, competing jurisdictions, voluntary restriction by binding rules, and a creed-shaped in-group that makes freedom-preserving software economically competitive. The frontier is not whether people need coordination — they do. It is whether coordination can become binding enough to defeat collective-action traps while remaining plural, contestable, and cheap to leave. Libertarianism has not shown that it can; centralizing the answer does not solve the problem, it hands the problem control of the system. He is not an apostate reporting from outside. He is working on the tradition's own unsolved problem.
