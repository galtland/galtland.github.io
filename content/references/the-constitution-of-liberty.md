# The Constitution of Liberty

> [[the-constitution-of-liberty|The Constitution of Liberty]] (1960) is [[friedrich-a-hayek|F. A. Hayek]]'s systematic statement of the philosophy of freedom — the mature, constructive counterpart to the warning of [[road-to-serfdom|The Road to Serfdom]].

## Liberty as the absence of arbitrary coercion

Hayek defines liberty negatively and precisely. It is not power, wealth, or the range of one's options, but a relation between persons: the condition of not being subject to the arbitrary will of another. The state he is after is

> "that condition of men in which coercion of some by others is reduced as much as is possible in society"
>
> — [[friedrich-a-hayek|F. A. Hayek]], [[the-constitution-of-liberty|The Constitution of Liberty]]

and its opposite is "the state in which a man is not subject to coercion by the arbitrary will of another or others" turned around. Defining freedom this way lets Hayek separate it cleanly from the confusions — freedom as ability, as political participation, as freedom from want — that let the word be turned against itself.

## The argument from ignorance

The distinctive move of the book is that its case for liberty rests not on knowing how freedom will be used but on *not* knowing. Because no mind can foresee which experiments, ideas, and adaptations will turn out to matter, a society that leaves individuals free to try things discovers more than any planner could design. Hayek puts the point sharply:

> "If we knew how freedom would be used, the case for it would largely disappear."
>
> — [[friedrich-a-hayek|F. A. Hayek]], [[the-constitution-of-liberty|The Constitution of Liberty]]

This is the [[knowledge-problem|knowledge problem]] turned from a critique of central planning into a positive defence of freedom, and it is why liberty for Hayek is bound up with [[spontaneous-order|spontaneous order]] — the beneficial patterns that arise from many free actions no one coordinated.

## The rule of law

Liberty's political embodiment, for Hayek, is the **rule of law**: government bound by general, prospective, equally applied rules rather than by particular commands aimed at particular people. When the state acts through known rules that apply to everyone including itself, coercion is confined and predictable, and the citizen can plan his own life inside a stable framework; when it acts by discretionary command, the same power that could protect liberty destroys it. The wiki develops this in [[hayek-on-rule-of-law|Hayek on the Rule of Law]] — the standard against which a programmable, discretionary monetary authority is judged.

## Applications, and "Why I Am Not a Conservative"

More than half the book applies the principle to concrete policy — labour unions, monetary policy, taxation, housing and rent control, education, social security, and the welfare state — showing how each is to be judged by whether it expands arbitrary coercion or confines it. The book closes with its most-quoted section, the postscript *Why I Am Not a Conservative*, in which Hayek refuses the conservative label: the liberal, he argues, faces an open future and welcomes change he cannot foresee, where the conservative distrusts it; the two share a suspicion of the socialist's rationalist planning but part on whether the direction of change, rather than change itself, is what matters.

## Place in This Wiki

The book is the constructive half of the wiki's Hayek cluster: where *The Road to Serfdom* warns where planning leads, *The Constitution of Liberty* sets out the positive alternative — a free order under the rule of law, defended from the fact of human ignorance rather than from any claim to superior knowledge. It anchors the classical-liberal, limited-government pole of the wiki's argument, distinct from the [[market-anarchism-and-private-law|anarcho-capitalist]] wing that would go further than Hayek's minimal but real state.
