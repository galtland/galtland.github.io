# The Conscience of a Hacker

> [[conscience-of-a-hacker|The Conscience of a Hacker]] is the 1986 manifesto Loyd Blankenship — "The Mentor" — wrote shortly after his arrest and published in *Phrack*. In a few hundred words it turned a police-blotter stereotype into a moral self-portrait: the hacker as the curious mind a bored school and a criminalizing establishment could not hold, judged for what he could do rather than who he was.

## What the Manifesto Says

The text is a monologue answering the adults who see only "Another one got caught today". Its speaker walks through the formation of a hacker — boredom in school, the discovery that a computer "does what I want it to," the door that opens onto the online world — punctuated by the refrain "They're all alike." The accusation is then reversed: the world that calls hackers criminals is the one that builds bombs and lies about it. The core of the ethic is its confession:

> "Yes, I am a criminal. My crime is that of curiosity. My crime is that of judging people by what they say and think, not what they look like. My crime is that of outsmarting you, something that you will never forgive me for."
>
> — The Mentor (Loyd Blankenship), “The Conscience of a Hacker”

Two further claims give the manifesto its lasting shape. First, the online world is a meritocracy of mind — "We exist without skin color, without nationality, without religious bias… and you call us criminals." Second, suppression cannot work at the level of individuals: "You may stop this individual, but you can't stop us all…"

## Why It Matters in This Wiki

The manifesto predates the [[cypherpunk|cypherpunk]] movement by half a decade, but it is the clearest early statement of the culture the cypherpunks inherited and then armed: the conviction that exploring, learning, and speaking in code are not crimes, and that the state's attempt to criminalize knowledge misreads both the people and the technology. Its "you can't stop us all" is the social premise behind [[code-as-speech|code as speech]] and the later legal battles over publishing cryptography: suppressing a distributed practice of mind is a losing game. Read it as the moral prologue to the movement whose technical program the wiki traces from PGP through Bitcoin.
